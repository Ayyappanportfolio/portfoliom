    01Problem Framing

    A success-rate number that didn't match how it felt to use

    The Signal Gap

    The metric said one thing, the business team said another

    65% success is a number a dashboard is happy with and a business team still complains about. I treated that gap itself as the research question, rather than assuming the chatbot was simply "fine" because the topline number looked acceptable.

    Internal Stakeholder Input

    Three pain points from the team using it day to day

    • Limited visibility into what the chatbot could actually do.
    • An experience gap on the business side — the internal team itself wasn't trained or onboarded on the product.
    • Usability friction that made even simple interactions feel harder than they should.
    EVIDENCE GAP — this audit's research base was internal business-team input and direct product evaluation, not moderated interviews with external Verizon business customers. No customer-facing interview transcripts or survey data were part of this engagement's scope.
    02Audit Framework

    Building a lens before forming an opinion

    Synthesizing the Internal Signal

    Three complaints, one root pattern

    Pattern → Insight: visibility, internal readiness, and usability friction all point at the same root — the chatbot was evaluated on task completion, never on comprehension or confidence. Read against the internal team's own framing — "customers don't know what this bot can actually help with," said with the defensiveness of a team whose dashboard says the product works — the pattern held: nobody had ever measured comprehension or confidence, only completion.

    EVIDENCE GAP — this synthesis is built from internal business-team input and direct product evaluation, not customer-facing interviews. An audit of this scope doesn't produce a customer persona or a stage-by-stage journey map — it evaluates the product against defined criteria, not an individual's path through it. That's a legitimate difference in deliverable, not a step skipped.
    Problem Definition

    From a defensible metric to an indefensible experience

    • §1Symptom. Business-team complaints despite a 65% success rate.
    • §2Problem. The chatbot is evaluated on task completion, not on whether users understand what happened or what to do next.
    • §3Root cause. No structured UX evaluation had ever been run against the product — only the completion metric existed.
    Point of view"A good user experience doesn't typically happen by accident. It's the result of careful planning, deep-dive research, analysis, investment of time and money, and continuous improvement."
    The Framework

    The direct answer: build the missing lens

    No structured evaluation existed → need a lens broader than task completion → opportunity: a named, criteria-based framework → solution: Direct, Orient & Guide, Engage, Inform, Connect.

    FIG. 02 — Direct, Orient & Guide, Engage, Inform, Connect — the framework built to evaluate before recommending anything
    • §1Direct — are users routed effectively to and within the right channel?
    • §2Orient & Guide — do the key pages orient and guide users effectively?
    • §3Engage — are users effectively engaged to drive conversion?
    • §4Inform — are users sufficiently informed to perform tasks?
    • §5Connect — are users successfully able to accomplish tasks without challenges?
    03Competitive Research

    Learning the category before recommending anything in it

    Category Research

    Mapping the solution space before naming a fix

    Before proposing what Verizon's chatbot should become, I mapped what a chatbot even is across the spectrum — rule-based versus conversational AI. A rule-based chatbot operates on predefined rules with static responses; conversational AI simulates genuine understanding via NLP, NLU, NLG, and deep learning. That distinction set the ceiling on what "Inform" and "Connect" could realistically achieve without a platform-level change, not just a UI one.

    FIG. 03 — Rule-based versus conversational AI — the ceiling on what a redesign alone could fix

    Industry best practices were brought in as design principles, not decoration: omni-channel and cross-channel continuity, automatic fallback so a misunderstood query never dead-ends, a multimodal interface, and a deliberate greeting/mascot design.

    FIG. 04 — The best practices used as design principles, not just inspiration
    Competitive Benchmarking

    A sourced competitive gap analysis, in place of a formal matrix

    EVIDENCE GAP — no formal impact/effort matrix was produced for this audit. In its place, the competitive teardown below functioned as the de facto prioritization — capabilities every competitor had that Verizon's bot didn't.

    Verizon's own VirtualAssistant was benchmarked directly against AT&T, T-Mobile, and Vodafone across eleven concrete capabilities — from contextual user history to predictive issue resolution to IoT device integration — sourced and cited rather than asserted from memory.

    FIG. 05 — Verizon against AT&T, T-Mobile, and Vodafone, feature by feature, sourced and cited
    TakeawayEvery competitor had features Verizon's bot didn't. Knowing exactly which ones, and why they mattered, is what turns "we should be more like the competition" into a prioritized list.
    04Scored Evaluation

    Testing the existing product against the framework, honestly

    Applying the Framework

    Not a user session — a scored evaluation against the framework

    EVIDENCE GAP — no moderated usability session with recruited participants was run. The evaluation here was applying the Direct/Orient & Guide/Engage/Inform/Connect framework to the current chatbot directly — a structured expert evaluation, not a user test. I'm naming that distinction rather than letting the word "test" imply something it wasn't.
    Findings

    The 65% reframed

    Scored against the framework, the 65% success rate stopped looking like a passing grade. It looked like a product that could technically resolve two out of three interactions while still leaving users unsure what it could do, unclear on next steps, and unable to self-serve with confidence — exactly the gap the business team had been describing anecdotally, now backed by a structured evaluation instead of a feeling.

    FIG. 08 — The rest of the framework applied, and the real next steps handed back to the team
    05Recommendations

    Making "Inform" and "Connect" concrete, not theoretical

    Recommended Entry Experience

    Popular topics first, not an empty prompt

    The entry point leads with a popular-topics grid — upgrade order, latest deals, check status, billing and payments, manage plan, manage account — instead of an empty text box asking the user to guess what the bot can help with. A visible settings panel and an upfront, honest disclosure that the user is talking to a digital assistant address the "Direct" and "Inform" gaps directly.

    FIG. 06 — The VZ Bot entry experience: popular topics first, settings visible, no guessing what it can do
    Recommended Bill-Pay Flow

    Bill-pay, mapped step by step

    Select bill→ Review amount→ Choose/add payment method→ Confirm

    Billing and payments — one of the highest-stakes tasks — got mapped as a full conversational flow: select a bill from pending or overdue, see it broken out with amounts, choose a saved payment method or add a new one, and confirm. Every step keeps the user informed of exactly where they are, which is precisely what "Connect" was measuring for.

    FIG. 07 — The bill-pay flow, mocked step by step: select, review, pay, confirm
    Recommendation Mockups

    The recommendation artifacts handed back to the team

    EVIDENCE GAP — these mockups are the audit's recommendation artifacts, not a documented low-to-high fidelity exploration — the record available for this case study doesn't preserve intermediate design rounds between recommendation and shipped build.
    TakeawayAn audit finding like "users aren't sufficiently informed" is easy to nod along to and hard to act on. A mocked-up bill-pay flow makes it unambiguous what "informed" should actually look like on screen — and it's what the redesign in Section 06 was built from.
    06Redesign & Launch

    The audit's findings became the brief — and the build

    From Recommendation to Build

    The recommendation mockups shipped, largely as designed

    The popular-topics entry point and the bill-pay conversational flow from Section 05 went into visual design and were built into the live Business Support chatbot — closing the "Inform" and "Connect" gaps the audit identified, rather than leaving them as a report on a shelf.

    EVIDENCE GAP — the record available for this case study doesn't include a separate moderated usability round on the shipped redesign, or specific post-launch metrics — stated directly rather than filled in with placeholder numbers.

    Key Learnings

    • A healthy metric can hide an unhealthy experience
      65% success rate and "users don't trust this" were both true at the same time. The framework is what reconciled them.
    • Define the rubric before forming the opinion
      Five named dimensions turned subjective critique into findings someone else could independently verify.
    • Cite the competition, don't just gesture at it
      A sourced, feature-by-feature comparison against AT&T, T-Mobile, and Vodafone carries weight that "competitors are better" never will.
    • An audit's real deliverable is the next phase's brief
      The framework and findings didn't just get filed — they became the direct input to a redesign that was built and shipped. An audit told honestly still ends somewhere real.

    The through-line: the most useful thing I delivered wasn't a mockup. It was a framework rigorous enough that Verizon's team built from it directly — and one they can reuse on the next feature.
