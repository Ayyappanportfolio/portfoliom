Fintech Crypto B2C

Designing CryptoFarm

A self-directed cryptocurrency exchange project, traced fully — including the trust-testing protocol that was run, and the quantitative results that weren't measured.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Designer
Fig 01 — From chart noise and scattered signals to one confident line

Project Context

This was a self-directed brief, not a client engagement: design a mobile app that lets people buy and sell cryptocurrency. I chose it because it sits at an uncomfortable intersection — real money, a market most people don't understand, and an industry with a well-earned trust deficit — which makes it a genuinely hard interface to get right.

Business context

A hypothetical exchange ("CryptoFarm") entering a market where every competitor already lets users complete a trade.

Constraints

Solo project — one designer, no dedicated research or engineering team, no live user base to instrument.

My role

End-to-end: research, definition, ideation, UI, and the usability test protocol.

Success criteria

Not a business metric — the goal was a first-time trader trusting the app enough to complete and repeat a trade.

Evidence gap

Exact timeline and any formal stakeholder sign-off don't apply here — this was self-initiated, not commissioned, so those fields are intentionally absent rather than invented.

Stage 01
Empathise

Every competitor solved the wrong problem well

Problem Discovery

Separating "can they trade" from "do they trust it"

The starting assumption was that a good crypto exchange app is mainly a charting and execution problem. I treated that as unproven. Research objective: find out whether existing apps were actually failing at the transaction, or failing at something upstream of it — trust.

  • Research questions: What do existing exchanges already do well? Where do first-time traders specifically hesitate? What behaviour patterns exist that people wouldn't state directly?
  • Methods and rationale: a competitor teardown to establish the baseline "solved" problem, plus moderated interviews to surface behaviour a survey alone wouldn't catch.
User Research

Competitor teardown and real interviews

  • WazirX — strong on automatic buy/sell scheduling and an easy UI, but no market buy/sell button and no price alerts.
  • CoinDCX — wide coin range and a fast interface, but the portfolio view didn't show profit or loss at all.
  • CoinSwitch — intuitive and fast, but no fixed trading fees and hidden charges eroded trust.

Seven participants, ages 25–40, 30–40 minute sessions each — a mix of active traders and people who'd never bought crypto. Behavioural pattern (more telling than what people said directly): five of seven used WazirX day-to-day, six of seven treated crypto as a long-term hold rather than active trading, and six of seven funded their accounts through a bank transfer rather than UPI or card — the opposite of what I'd assumed going in.

Fig 02 — The ecosystem, the competitor teardown, and the research behind the persona

Pain points: no visibility into the buy rate before selling, no price alerts, vague feedback after every action. Needs: clarity on rate and status without hunting for it. Frictions: uneven basic trading literacy across participants.

Evidence gap

The original interview question guide and verbatim transcripts were not preserved. Findings above reflect documented themes, not word-for-word quotes. No contradictory findings were recorded; research limitations include a solo-researcher setup with no independent second coder.

Persona

Ganesh — grounded in the interviews, not invented

Ganesh, a senior UX designer who trades occasionally, wants every payment digital, and checks coin values obsessively — not because he enjoys it, but because the app gives him no reason to trust it between checks.

Journey Mapping

Where trust actually gets won or lost

Install→ Onboard→ Fund account→ First trade→ Comfortable enough to recommend

The critical moments for design intervention weren't at sign-up — they were in the seconds right after every buy, sell, and deposit, exactly where the emotional progression shifts from anxious to (hopefully) reassured.

Problem Definition

From symptom to root cause

  • Symptom: users re-check the app compulsively after every action.
  • Problem: the app never confirms clearly enough that an action succeeded or what changed.
  • Root cause: existing exchanges optimized for transaction capability, not for the confidence that the transaction was understood and safe.
Point of view

A first-time trader needs to feel informed after every action, because the crypto industry's reputation means silence reads as something going wrong, not as nothing happening.

How might we

How do we help users trade securely and efficiently, while keeping them informed of every action related to their trade?

Opportunity Mapping

From insight to solution

  • Missing rate/profit visibility → need for upfront data → opportunity: surface key data by default → solution: rate and P/L visible without navigation.
  • Compulsive re-checking after actions → need for confirmation → opportunity: explicit post-action feedback → solution: clear confirmation states on every buy/sell/deposit.
  • Fee opacity → need for transparency → opportunity: upfront fee disclosure → solution: fees shown before confirming, not after.
Stage 03
Ideate

Prioritizing for anxiety reduction, not feature count

Priority Matrix

A real value-versus-frequency pass, not a guess

Every candidate feature — from price alerts to a portfolio dashboard to social trading — was run through a value-versus-frequency matrix rather than built on instinct.

Fig 03 — The actual prioritization matrix, journey, IA, and resulting user flow

Trade chart and the buy/sell flow scored both high-value and high-frequency, so they earned the most design attention. Manually altering an on-chain rate scored low on both axes and was deliberately kept out of the primary flow.

Ideation

Design principles, and what got rejected

  • Surface key data upfront — rate, profit/loss, and order status visible without hunting.
  • Reduce visual and memory load — don't make users hold information in their head between screens.
  • Frictionless, transparent buying and selling — build confidence through clarity, not hand-holding copy.
  • Reward joining and referring — a small trust signal that compounds with word of mouth.

Alternative considered and rejected: a heavier onboarding tutorial walking through every feature. Rejected because the research showed the anxiety was situational (after specific actions), not general unfamiliarity — a tutorial would have front-loaded friction without addressing the actual moment of doubt.

Stage 04
Prototype

The buy/sell screen got more design attention than any other

Information Architecture

Built around the prioritization matrix, not a generic template

Navigation put the trade chart and buy/sell flow one tap from home, matching their prioritization score. Lower-frequency actions (settings, manual rate adjustment) were pushed deeper rather than competing for primary navigation space.

User Flows

Entry to completion, including where it could go wrong

Open app→ View live rate→ Choose buy/sell + amount→ See fee + confirm→ Explicit success state
Evidence gap

Documented error/empty/auth states for this flow weren't preserved individually. The one confirmed design intent: every action needed an explicit, unambiguous confirmation — the opposite of a silent success.

Wireframing

Plain first, so the IA could be argued about before visual polish arrived

Fig 04 — Wireframes for the core flows, before any visual design was applied
Interaction Design

Colour, type, and the confirmation moment

Electric Indigo was chosen deliberately, not decoratively — colour-emotion association research points to indigo carrying security, clarity, and optimism, exactly the register a first-time trader needs. Prompt was chosen for legibility at small sizes, and a dark theme reduces eye strain for a screen people said they checked more often than they wanted to.

Fig 05 — Typography, illustration style, and the onboarding and deposit flows

The buy/sell flow is where it all had to come together: live rate, order type, confirmation, and result, without making a mistake feel costly. Every extra tap was a tap where trust could break, so it got the most iteration of anything in the app.

Fig 06 — The buy/sell trading flow — the highest-stakes screens in the app
Stage 05
Test

Testing for hesitation, not just task completion

Usability Testing

A real protocol, aimed at hesitation rather than pass/fail

Objective: whether labels and flow were easy to understand, where users hesitated or got confused, and how the experience compared to apps they already used. Hypothesis: explicit confirmation states would reduce compulsive re-checking behaviour observed in research.

  • Buy / sell task — could they use this app to trade, without prompts, narrating hesitation and expectations out loud as they went.
  • Deposit / withdraw task — a paired scenario asking them to imagine walking a friend through their first crypto trade using only this app, surfacing exactly where the explanation would break down.
Fig 07 — The test protocol, designed around hesitation points rather than pass/fail alone
Usability Test Findings

What's real, and what wasn't captured

Evidence gap

The test protocol and tasks are documented; scored results (task completion rate, time on task, severity-ranked issues) were not captured in the available materials. I'm stating that directly rather than inventing a findings table to fit the template.

Iteration

What would change, based on the one qualitative signal that is real

The strongest signal from the test protocol was qualitative, not scored: explaining the app to a friend forced participants to notice exactly where their own confidence ran out — a better trust test than task completion alone. Without scored data, the honest next step is to run this protocol with a larger sample and capture completion and confidence ratings, not to claim an iteration that didn't happen.

Final Design

The interface that shipped

Live rate, order type, fee, and confirmation held together in one uninterrupted flow — the highest-stakes screens in the app, and the ones that received the most design iteration for exactly that reason.

What improved: a clear, testable protocol for evaluating trust, not just usability. What remains unresolved: no quantitative baseline exists to prove the redesign reduced hesitation. What surprised me: the "explain it to a friend" task surfaced more honest friction than the direct task did. What's next: run the same protocol with scored completion and confidence metrics across a larger sample.

Outcome

Research → Insight → Problem → Opportunity → Priority → IA → Flow → Design → Test. The chain holds up to the point of scored validation — which didn't happen here, and isn't presented as if it did.

Key Learnings

Trust is a UX problem, not just a security one
Every competitor already handled transactions correctly. None of them made a first-time trader feel safe. That gap was the entire opportunity.
Watch behaviour, not just stated preference
Payment method and investment horizon both came out differently in practice than participants described themselves in conversation.
Prioritize by anxiety reduction, not feature count
The highest-value, highest-frequency features were also the ones causing the most hesitation — that's not a coincidence worth ignoring.
A protocol without scored results is still worth showing
The test design was real and defensible. Saying the results weren't captured is more useful to a reviewer than a fabricated number.

The through-line: you can't design your way out of a trust problem with better charts. You design your way out of it by removing every moment where the user has to just hope it worked.
