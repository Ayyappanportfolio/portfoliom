An Honest UX Audit of M2P

Not a product case study — a strategy one. After two days actually sitting with the product and design teams, the real finding wasn't a broken screen. It was a design function with no seat at the table.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Strategy, M2P Fintech
Fig 01 — Starting from the business model, not the interface

M2P sits at the intersection of finance and technology — banking-as-a-service infrastructure for banks, NBFCs, lending startups, gold loan providers, forex houses, and eCommerce merchants. Before proposing anything about design, I mapped the actual business: who M2P's partners are, what it sells, who pays for it, and where the money and risk actually sit.

The Project

This wasn't commissioned as a product redesign. It started as two days of quick findings from sitting with the product team and the design team, and it grew into a fuller strategic assessment: where is UX actually positioned at M2P today, what is that costing the business, and what's a realistic first move rather than a five-year transformation plan nobody will fund?

Step 01
The Business

Reading M2P as a business before touching a single screen

A Business Model Canvas isn't a design artifact. It's the fastest way to find out whether a UX problem is actually a UX problem, or a business-model problem wearing a UI complaint.

Key partners span investors, banks, corporates, and eCommerce or third-party merchants. Core activities are security, platform R&D and maintenance, data analytics, algorithm development, and sales and support. The value proposition is squarely infrastructural: helping businesses accept digital payments efficiently through powerful Banking-as-a-Service APIs, with an emphasis on speed and reduced payment friction.

Customer segments told the real story: banks wanting to go digital-first, microfinance companies, gold loan providers, lending startups, forex houses, insurance companies, eCommerce merchants, and travellers — a genuinely wide B2B and B2B2C base, each with different stakes in whether M2P's products feel trustworthy and easy to integrate.

The takeawayA platform selling trust and infrastructure to banks has a much lower tolerance for "confusing but functional" UI than a consumer app does. That's a business fact, not a design opinion.
Step 02
The Case for UX

"UX cannot happen without users" — across three very different product lines

M2P's product suite spans Payments, Lending, and Banking — each with genuinely different users, contexts, and risk profiles. A generic design team without a unifying vision was always going to produce three inconsistent products.

  • Payments — wallets and cards, just-in-time funding, cross-border, gift, fleet, UPI, and acceptance.
  • Lending — consumer, corporate, SME, and credit card products.
  • Banking — accounts, deposits, and investments.
Fig 02 — Three product lines, one design function, and the goals it needed to serve

The case for investing in UX leadership rested on making that vision concrete: a unified customer experience across products that depend on each other, clear brand values, and scalability — not a slogan about "world-class design," but a specific, arguable position on what a UX head would actually be responsible for building and defending.

The takeaway"We need better UX" is not a business case. "Three product lines are shipping inconsistent experiences to the same bank customer" is.
Step 03
Honest Assessment

Placing M2P honestly on the UX maturity curve — stage 2 of 6

Rather than asserting "our UX needs work," I benchmarked M2P against the Nielsen Norman Group's UX maturity model — six stages from Absent to User-driven — and placed it honestly, not flatteringly.

Fig 03 — M2P assessed at "Limited": uneven, haphazard, aspirational

That assessment was backed by specifics, not vibes. Reviewing M2P's own live web portals directly — including its forex and default web-app portals — surfaced a consistent pattern: a unified visual experience in places, but standards that were plain, dated, and inconsistently applied; a lack of holistic and business context in the design process; and real gaps in what the team actually knew about its users' needs, goals, and pain points. Ongoing redesign work at the time (PRDs, a branding style guide) showed effort already underway — the gap was coordination, not motivation.

Fig 04 — What reviewing the actual live portals surfaced, threats included
Evidence gap

This assessment came from a two-day review and direct portal inspection, not a formal audit with scored criteria or a documented sample size. It's presented as what it was — a fast, honest first read — not a statistically rigorous study.

The takeawayNaming the maturity stage honestly — "Limited," not "developing" or some softer euphemism — is what makes a UX investment case credible to people who've heard vague design pitches before.
Step 04
The Plan

Redesign just one product — not the whole portfolio at once

The honest threats identified — disagreements among stakeholders, unclear goals, an ineffective action plan — pointed away from a big-bang transformation and toward a scoped, provable first step.

The plan started with specific people, not abstractions: learning directly from stakeholders like Vijay Subramanian and Ellappa Shanmugasundaram why a redesign was needed, who the users actually are and how they differ, how frequently they'd use the product, and — critically — what metrics would actually define success.

High impact · Low effort — the actual pick
  • Redesign one scoped product, with named stakeholders and metrics
High impact · High effort — rejected for now
  • Redesign the full three-product portfolio simultaneously
Low impact · Low effort
  • A cosmetic style-guide refresh with no process change
Low impact · High effort — rejected
  • A multi-year UX transformation program with no near-term proof point
Fig 05 — A plan built around specific people and specific questions, not a generic framework

From there: understand user goals through properly sampled research (novice and expert users, a real mix of demographics, not just whoever was easiest to reach), and prepare the team with a concrete UX checklist — identify the vital few tasks using an 80/20 lens, do a structured review, report and discuss issues, and build toward standardized, close-knit design and engineering integration rather than one-off fixes.

The takeaway"Redesign everything" is a plan nobody can be held accountable to. "Redesign one product, with named stakeholders and named metrics" is a plan that can actually succeed or fail visibly.
Step 05
Principles

"Don't make me think" doesn't stop applying just because the user is a bank

B2B UX in fintech gets treated as exempt from consumer design rigor more often than it should. The principles guiding this plan explicitly rejected that.

  • Steve Krug's core rule — let people accomplish their intended tasks as easily and directly as possible — applied to bank operators and compliance officers just as much as to end consumers.
  • Persuasion has an ethical line: seek consent, respect users' time and attention, and stay alert to the difference between straightforward recommendation and manipulation.
  • Deceptive patterns are a named risk, not a hypothetical one — dark patterns that trick users into auto-renewals, hidden subscriptions, or confusing settings erode exactly the trust a banking infrastructure company depends on.
Fig 06 — Why the stakes for good — and honest — design are specifically higher in fintech

Fintech sits at the intersection of finance and technology, competing with banks while depending on their trust and their rules. Good design earns loyalty and repeat use; bad design costs more than it saves, because a bad reputation in this industry is expensive to reverse. That's the case, in the end, for treating UX as a strategic differentiator rather than a cosmetic layer applied at the end.

The takeawayIn an industry where the customer is a bank and the end user is that bank's customer, deceptive design doesn't just risk a bad review — it risks the B2B relationship the entire business depends on.

Key Learnings

Start with the business model, not the backlog
Understanding M2P's actual partners, segments, and revenue model is what kept the UX recommendations grounded in the business, not just design taste.
Name the maturity stage honestly
Calling M2P's UX maturity "Limited" against a real external model carried more weight than any internal opinion could.
Scope down to one provable win
A plan to redesign one product, with named stakeholders and named metrics, is achievable. A plan to fix everything at once usually isn't.
B2B doesn't lower the ethical bar
Deceptive patterns cost a fintech infrastructure company more than most, because its entire business is built on institutional trust.

The through-line: this document didn't ship a screen. It made the case, with real evidence and real names, for why design needed a seat at the table before the next screen got shipped.
