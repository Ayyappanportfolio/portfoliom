Designing GO FASTag

A time-boxed design assignment for Kotak — the full trace, including the usability test that was planned properly and never got run.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Designer, M2P Fintech
Fig 01 — The actual brief: a basic app for buying and managing a personal FASTag

Project Context

This was a design assignment M2P's team took on for Kotak — a specific, bounded brief: design a basic mobile app that lets someone buy and manage a FASTag for their vehicle.

Constraints

No obligation to match Kotak's visual identity, personal FASTags only (not commercial), a fixed 360×640 viewport.

Timeline

A real, binding time constraint — confirmed by the project's own decision to skip planned usability testing rather than delay.

Existing ecosystem

NETC issues FASTags officially; users encounter them through banks, standalone apps, and payment wallets, each covering a slice.

My role

End-to-end design within M2P's team, on assignment for Kotak.

Stage 01
Empathise

Nobody owns the FASTag experience end to end

Problem Discovery

Mapping the ecosystem before assuming the gap

The assumption to check: that FASTag apps fail at features. Mapping the ecosystem first suggested otherwise — NETC issues the tag officially, but users encounter it through banks, standalone apps, and payment wallets, each covering a slice, none covering the whole trip.

User Research

Competitor teardown plus seven real interviews

Fragmentation showed up directly in the research: "Many people tend to buy Fastag from banks or banking apps mainly to get rid of KYC." "There seems to be no app that comprehensively addresses all the needs — car and travel — of a highway traveller." "Users are still not aware of many things around Fastag."

  • Paytm (4.6★) — easy recharge for existing users, but cluttered, no support for tags bought elsewhere.
  • ICICI iMobile (4.3★) — could manage multiple tags, but dated UI, no support path when something breaks.
  • Park+ FasTag (4.5★) — covered more car-related needs in one place, but OTP delays and poor customer care.
Fig 02 — What Paytm, iMobile, and Park+ already got right, and where each still failed users

Seven participants, ages 28–40, frequent highway travellers from Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities: most weren't sure how much to recharge and just topped up a random amount, several were confused about why KYC couldn't be done through Aadhaar, and almost nobody could explain how they'd track a transaction after the fact.

Evidence gap

The full interview guide wasn't preserved beyond the themes above; no quantitative survey ran alongside these interviews.

Persona

Sunilkumar, a senior lead at a software company in Bangalore, married with two kids, plans road trips with his family and wants to prepare everything — including tolls — in advance rather than deal with surprises mid-trip.

Problem Definition

From transaction to trip

  • Symptom: confusion about recharge amount, KYC, and past transactions.
  • Problem: FASTag apps treat the tag as the product, not the trip it's meant to support.
  • Root cause: every existing app was built around the transaction (buy, recharge) rather than the traveller's actual planning need.
How might we

How might we help users make their trips frictionless and efficient, and help them stay informed of everything related to the trip and the vehicle?

Opportunity Mapping

The reframe's direct consequence

Recharge uncertainty → need to know costs ahead of time → opportunity: estimate tolls before recharging → solution: the Toll Estimator, detailed next.

Stage 03
Ideate

One feature answered a question no competitor asked

Ideation

Five ideas, one genuinely novel

  • Toll Estimator — estimate toll charges for an upcoming trip, answering a need none of the three competitors addressed.
  • Reminders and alerts — reduce memory load for balance and renewal.
  • Simple onboarding — frictionless first-time sign-up.
  • Flexible, transparent payments — deals surfaced upfront (UPI, 5% off), not buried.
  • Context-sensitive help — FAQ content placed where confusion actually happens.

Alternative considered and rejected: a generic rewards program mirroring Paytm's model. Rejected because the research pointed at planning anxiety, not reward-seeking behaviour — a rewards layer would have addressed a problem participants didn't actually have.

Priority Matrix

Value versus frequency, applied honestly

Estimating tolls, recharging, viewing transactions, and speed-limit info ranked high-value and high-frequency — the core of the app. Buying a FASTag, insurance, and tracking ranked high-value but lower-frequency.

Top-level navigation surfaced the Toll Estimator, Recharge, and Transaction history — the highest-value, highest-frequency items — rather than defaulting to a generic account-first structure.

Wireframing

What's preserved: final wireframes, not the exploration

Evidence gap

Earlier low-fidelity exploration rounds weren't preserved separately from the final wireframe set shown in Stage 05.

Interaction Design

Colour chosen deliberately, freedom or not

The brief explicitly freed the design from Kotak's visual identity, so colour was chosen deliberately: a blue-purple primary to signal trust and stability, and an orange-peel secondary to carry optimism, clarity, and cheerfulness — both noted honestly as subject to change based on user testing, not treated as final the moment they looked good.

Recharge and transaction history got the same direct treatment the research called for: recharge in as few steps as the payment method allows, and a transaction view organized so a trip's toll history is actually findable afterward.

Fig 07 — The recharge flow and transaction history, built for exactly the confusion research surfaced
The takeawayFreedom from a brand guideline is still a design decision waiting to be made deliberately — "blue and orange" needed the same emotional reasoning "on-brand" would have forced anyway.
Stage 05
Test

The test plan existed. The time to run it didn't.

Usability Testing

A real, unrun plan — stated plainly

Objectives: ease of use, whether labels and features were understandable, whether users could and would complete key tasks, and how the app compared to what people already used. Key tasks: buying a new FASTag without prompting, recharging with clear expectations set up front, and reviewing a shared transaction with a friend to see how naturally someone could explain their own spending. A BERT (bipolar emotional response) test was planned to capture how the experience felt, not just whether it worked.

Fig 08 — The wireframes, and the note included rather than hidden: testing was skipped due to time constraints
Usability Test Findings

None — by project constraint, not oversight

Evidence gap

No usability session was run, so there are no findings to report. This is stated directly in the original project notes, not glossed over here.

Final Result

What's real, and what's still a hypothesis

MetricResult
Task completion / time on taskNot measured — test not run

Toll Estimator — competitor coverageConfirmed: 0 of 3 competitors offer it

What's real: a fully reasoned design with a documented rationale at every decision point. What's not: any evidence it actually works for a user, because the one step that would prove that was the step time ran out on.

Future Scope

  • Run the usability test plan that was already written — buy, recharge, and shared-transaction tasks — before treating any screen as final.
  • Validate the colour palette against real users rather than emotional theory alone.
  • Extend the Toll Estimator with real-time route data.

Key Learnings

A fragmented ecosystem is the actual competitor
Paytm, iMobile, and Park+ weren't bad products. None of them owned the whole trip, and that gap was the opportunity.
Reframe around the goal, not the object
"Manage a FASTag" and "stay informed about your trip" point to different apps, even though they cover the same screens.
Creative freedom still needs a rationale
No brand constraint meant more responsibility to justify colour, not less.
Say what you didn't get to test
A real project has real constraints. Naming the one that mattered here is more useful than pretending it didn't exist.

The through-line: the best idea in this project — the Toll Estimator — didn't come from studying FASTag apps. It came from asking what Sunilkumar actually needed on the morning of his trip.
