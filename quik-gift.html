Peer recognition, inside Microsoft Teams

Designing QuikGift

A peer-to-peer recognition platform built to live inside Microsoft Teams — the full trace, including exactly where the process stopped before a formal user test.

EnterpriseHR TechB2B
Ayyappan Rajendiran · UX Designer
Fig. 01 — QuikGift: a Microsoft Azure-based B2C corporate rewards application

QuikGift is a corporate rewards platform for sending and receiving recognition between colleagues — integrated directly into Microsoft Teams so it lives inside the tool people already use all day, rather than becoming one more app nobody opens.

Business context
Employee recognition fails quietly when it happens outside daily workflow — especially in remote/hybrid teams. That absence compounds into lower motivation and productivity.
Existing ecosystem
No unified recognition tool — appreciation happened ad hoc, outside any system that could track or reinforce it.
Constraints
Had to work as a Microsoft Teams-embedded experience, not a standalone app competing for attention.
My role
UX Designer — stakeholder research, persona development, competitive analysis, and the shipped design.

01 — Research & Discovery

Four business goals that all point at the same friction

Problem Discovery

Testing whether "add a rewards app" was even the right frame

The assumption to check: that recognition was failing for lack of a good enough rewards app. Stakeholder input suggested something narrower and more specific — recognition was failing because it required leaving the tools people already worked in.

User Research

Four goals, three real constraints, three user groups

  • Workplace Engagement Catalyst — foster a culture of appreciation, empowering colleagues to recognize each other seamlessly.
  • Integrated Recognition Hub — a fully embedded solution within Microsoft Teams, frictionless without leaving daily workflow.
  • Holistic Rewards Ecosystem — peer-to-peer recognition plus real analytics and reporting for HR and management.
  • Recognition Experience Expert — tailored, personalized reward options that understand workplace dynamics.

Three real challenges sat underneath those goals: a workplace culture that doesn't already prioritize recognition is hard to shift by adding software alone; employee data and reward distribution raise real compliance and privacy questions; and a reliable payment processing system means genuine technical complexity.

Fig. 02 — The real constraints: culture, compliance, and technical complexity

Three distinct groups needed this to work for different reasons: employees sending and receiving recognition day to day, managers recognizing their teams and tracking engagement, and HR overseeing the program and needing to prove it was working.

Evidence gapThe original stakeholder interview guide and count of participants weren't preserved beyond the goals and challenges documented above.

Problem Definition

"Better visibility" is two different features

  • Symptom: both a manager and an HR lead ask for "better visibility" into recognition.
  • Problem: that phrase means a fast personal view for one and a defensible analytics rollup for the other.
  • Root cause: no existing tool served both altitudes from the same underlying data.
How might weHow might we make recognition fast enough for a manager's daily habit, while giving HR the aggregated proof they need to defend the program?
Opportunity Mapping

One data model, named as the opportunity early

"Visibility" meaning two different things → need for both a fast personal view and a rolled-up analytical one → opportunity: one shared data model → solution: a personal recognition hub and a separate HR dashboard, detailed in Section 04.

03 — Prioritization & Ideation

None of the closest competitors were actually competitors

Priority Matrix

Three real strengths, none of them complete

Peer Fives does social recognition well but offers little beyond it. Viva Insights (Microsoft's own product) has strong data insight but isn't engaging day to day. Starbucks has neither social recognition nor productivity insight, but nobody does gamification and repeat engagement better.

vs. Peer Fives vs. Viva Insights vs. Starbucks
Fig. 05 — No single competitor covered social recognition, data insight, and gamification at once
Ideation

Borrowing deliberately from outside the category

That comparison directly shaped the brief: build the social recognition layer Peer Fives does well, back it with the analytics rigor Viva Insights users expect from Microsoft's own ecosystem, and borrow enough of Starbucks' gamification instinct to make recognition something people want to keep doing.

Alternative considered and rejected: building only the HR analytics layer first, treating the personal recognition experience as a later phase. Rejected because the research showed recognition dies without a fast, frictionless entry point — building the dashboard first would have had nothing to report on.

TakeawayThe right competitive set isn't always your direct competitors. Starbucks taught this project more about repeat engagement than any HR tech product did.
04 — Design & Prototyping

A dashboard for Ram, a landing page for Jenita — one product

Information Architecture

Split by altitude, not by feature

One underlying data model, two entry points: a personal hub for fast, individual recognition, and an HR dashboard for the aggregated, exportable view.

User Flows

Open Teams hub→Pick colleague + reward→Send→HR views rollup on dashboard
Wireframing

Low-fidelity first, to test the split before styling it

Wireframes stayed low-fidelity first specifically to test whether one information architecture could genuinely serve both the fast, personal act of sending recognition and the aggregated, analytical view HR needed.

Interaction Design

Two interfaces, one visual system

The shipped design split cleanly: a personal app landing page — "The Peer-to-Peer Gifting Hub" — as the fast, human entry point, and a separate analytics dashboard giving HR and managers the aggregated, exportable view Ram Prasath needed to defend the program's ROI.

Fig. 07 — The personal landing page and the HR dashboard, sharing one visual system
TakeawayServing two very different jobs-to-be-done didn't mean building two products — it meant designing two entry points into the same underlying data.
05 — Validation

User testing was scoped into the process — the honest scorecard

Usability Testing

Named as the final stage — not run within this documented scope

Evidence gapThe design process for QuikGift explicitly named user testing as its final stage, alongside empathy, define, ideate, and design. No session — participants, tasks, scores — was documented within the material available for this case study, and I'm not backfilling results I can't verify.
What Would Make Testing Meaningful

The structure that was actually built for it

Two personas with genuinely different success criteria, a competitive analysis that named specific, testable feature gaps, and two distinct interfaces — the personal hub and the HR dashboard — that could each be evaluated against the persona they were built for. That's the scaffolding a real test would have been run against.

TakeawayA test is only as good as the personas and criteria it's run against. Getting Jenita's and Ram's actual needs right up front is what would have made that final testing stage worth running.

Key Learnings

Recognition fails at the point of friction, not intent

Nobody in this research lacked the desire to recognize colleagues. They lacked a fast enough way to do it inside their actual workflow.

The same word can hide two different features

"Visibility" meant a quick personal view for Jenita and a defensible analytics rollup for Ram — conflating them would have underserved both.

Borrow from outside your category on purpose

Starbucks isn't an HR platform, but its rewards program is a masterclass in repeat engagement.

Naming a test stage isn't the same as running one

The process document said "test." This case study only claims what that stage actually produced.

The through-line: QuikGift's real competition was never another rewards app. It was the extra ten seconds of effort that decides whether recognition happens at all.
