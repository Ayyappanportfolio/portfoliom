Fintech Remittance B2C

Rebuilding LuLu Money

Turning a 2018 remittance app for migrant workers into the foundation of a digital bank — the full trace, including where formal usability testing never happened.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Designer, LuLu Financial
Fig 01 — What I inherited: a remittance app that worked, built in 2018, showing every year of it

Project Context

LuLu Money is powered by LuLu Exchange — a real financial services company with over 200 branches worldwide, moving money to more than 170 countries. This wasn't a concept redesign. It was a live product, with real customers depending on it to get money to their families, that had to keep working while I rebuilt it underneath them.

Business context

The 2018 hybrid app covered one product — account-to-account transfer. The business goal was a digital bank: forex, bill payments, wallets, wallet transfers.

Constraints

Six countries at once (UAE, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Bahrain, APAC), each with its own regulations and language expectations.

Existing ecosystem

Users ranged from blue-collar workers on shared Android devices to white-collar professionals managing investments — one app, very different comfort levels.

My role

UX Designer driving research, definition, ideation, and the redesigned flows across the rebuild.

Stage 01
Empathise

Starting with stakeholders, not users — deliberately

Problem Discovery

Business-alignment risk comes before interface risk

The assumption I refused to start with was "the UI needs modernizing." A rebuild this size fails first at the business-alignment layer, so the research plan opened with stakeholders across six markets before a single end-user session.

User Research

Stakeholders, personas, and 321 real reviews

Roughly eight hours across three sessions with stakeholders in UAE, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Bahrain, and the wider APAC business surfaced the real constraint immediately: "It's not customer centric — only account transfer, no western-money-like services. There's demand for more products." Stakeholders were also clear on who actually uses this app: migrant workers, blue and white collar, sending money home regularly to support families, education, and investments — many of them Android users with limited technical proficiency, most comfortable in WhatsApp, Facebook, and YouTube rather than banking apps.

Fig 02 — The sprint plan and what stakeholders told me before I spoke to a single end user

Two personas carried the rest of the research: Md Shahid Alam, a construction worker sending cash home on a tight schedule with low tolerance for friction, and Amit Sharma, a white-collar professional more comfortable with digital tools but just as unforgiving of a broken experience.

Fig 03 — Two personas, two very different relationships with the same app

Then the reviews — 321 of them, from the App Store and Play Store — let the actual complaints speak for themselves: "Why is this app not support the Arabic language." "The worst app ever... im not even able to open this... its showing a white screen the entire time." "I am putting my phone number in Qatar but is taking ages and still not able to send me OTP." Against Wise and Al Ansari Exchange, the pattern held: competitors had useful features but buried them in confusing hierarchies, or shipped sign-up flows that broke on login.

Fig 04 — What customers actually said, and where Wise and Al Ansari Exchange were also falling short
Evidence gap

The original stakeholder interview guide and full session transcripts weren't preserved beyond the themes above. No formal quantitative survey was run alongside the qualitative interviews and review mining.

Personas / Segments

Two named personas, plus three customer segments

I grouped the customer base honestly, because a rebuild aimed at everyone serves no one: the lapsed (people who now see LuLu Money as old-fashioned and moved to Liv or Western Union), the current (loyal and passively-engaged customers using it today), and the future (people who'd consider LuLu Money when the need arises).

Journey Mapping

Not documented as a formal journey map

Evidence gap

A stage-by-stage journey map with tracked emotions wasn't produced for this rebuild. The send-money flow (Stage 04) is where the equivalent critical moments were identified and designed for directly.

Problem Definition

"Not customer centric" is a headline, not a problem statement

  • Symptom: negative reviews citing crashes, missing Arabic support, broken OTP.
  • Problem: a single-product 2018 app trying to serve a multi-product, multi-market, multi-literacy user base.
  • Root cause: the app was never re-architected as the business expanded from remittance-only to a broader digital-bank ambition.
How might we

How might we rebuild LuLu Money into a digital-bank foundation without breaking the trust migrant workers already place in it to get money home reliably?

Opportunity Mapping

Every crucial problem paired with one clear answer

  • No product breadth beyond account transfer → introduce cash payout, mobile wallet, and bill payments as first-class flows, not add-ons.
  • Transaction status missing → a track-transaction feature for non-login users, plus an activity view with live status for logged-in users.
  • No way to manage beneficiaries → a dedicated navigation entry to add and manage recipients.
  • Multilingual support missing → language selection surfaced at onboarding and in settings, directly answering the Arabic-language complaints.

Stage 03
Ideate

Crucial, important, and not-really — the real priority pass

Priority Matrix

A genuine three-tier prioritization, not everything treated as urgent

Fig 06 — Sorting every flagged problem into crucial, important, and not really

Missing product breadth, unclear special rates, and no way to manage beneficiaries landed as crucial. A missing onboarding tutorial and no multi-channel sign-in landed as important but not urgent. A missing referral option and in-house push notifications landed as nice-to-haves that could wait.

Ideation

Direct problem-to-solution mapping, not open brainstorming

Evidence gap

A divergent brainstorming session with alternative concepts and rejected ideas wasn't documented separately — the process went directly from prioritized problem to a single scoped solution per item, shown in Stage 02's opportunity map. That's a real discipline choice (see Key Learnings), not an omission I'm hiding.

The one strategic idea that ran through every solution: leverage LuLu's actual advantage over purely digital competitors — its massive agent network — bridging online payment initiation with offline cash pickup, rather than pretending to be a digital-only challenger.

Stage 05
Test

The real test was six markets, not a usability lab

Usability Testing

No formal usability-testing round was run

Evidence gap

There was no moderated usability-testing framework — recruited participants, task scenarios, SUS scoring — for this rebuild. Stating that directly rather than presenting the market rollout as if it were a lab test.

What actually happened instead: the design shipped across four sprints — discover, define, ideate, design — with development and testing running continuously alongside the later sprints, into six live regulatory markets at once. That's a harsher, less controlled validation than a lab session, but it isn't the same thing, and this case study doesn't conflate the two.

Findings & Iteration

Feedback arrived continuously, not in a single round

The project's own framing going into rollout was the most honest thing to carry into this case study: design is never really done. From the first sprint onward, feedback kept arriving, and the product kept adapting to different languages and regulations across markets that don't share the same rules.

Final Result

Stated at the level it was actually measured

MetricBeforeAfterChange
App reliability complaints (reviews)Present — crashes, white screenNot re-measuredNo post-launch review audit conducted
Language coverageMissing Arabic, cited directlyMultilingual support addedAddressed by design, not re-verified with users
Task completion / SUSNot measuredNot measuredNo formal test run

What improved: product breadth, transaction visibility, and language coverage were addressed by design. What remains unresolved: no formal usability round confirmed these changes actually reduced the complaints seen in reviews. What's next: a real post-launch review audit and, ideally, the moderated usability round this rebuild shipped without.

Future Scope

  • Extend beyond money transfer into forex booking and bill payments as first-class products.
  • Build out the wallet and mobile wallet transfer experience the original goal called for.
  • Run the moderated usability round this rebuild shipped without, now that the core flows are live.

Key Learnings

Start with stakeholders when the risk is strategic
User interviews would have found UI problems. Stakeholder interviews found a business-model problem hiding behind them.
Segment before you personalize
The lapsed, the current, and the future customer needed different things from the same redesign.
A shipped market rollout isn't a usability test
Six regulatory markets validated that the product worked. It didn't validate whether the design decisions themselves were the right ones — that gap is real and worth naming.
Regulation and language are UX constraints, not edge cases
Designing for six markets at once meant treating compliance and localization as core requirements from sprint one.

The through-line: rebuilding a live financial product isn't about designing the ideal version from scratch. It's about knowing exactly which parts of the legacy experience were trust, not just friction, and protecting those while you fix everything else.
