Designing Preboarding

A single portal for the quiet weeks between accepting an offer and walking in on Day 1 — built around M2P Fintech's real brand story, for the gap before Day 1.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Designer · M2P Fintech
Fig 01 — Preboarding: a single landing screen, designed for the gap before Day 1

Project Context

The screen's own copy names the company: "M2P Solutions is one of the largest B2B Fintech/payments platform in India... expanding rapidly across Asia." That's the same M2P Fintech named as the client on the Design System case study elsewhere on this site — though the headline component above that paragraph still reads "Why 'Brand Name'?", an unswapped placeholder left in place. It asks a narrow question: between the moment someone accepts an offer and the moment they actually start, what's the one screen worth giving them?

Product

Preboarding — a portal for new hires to open before their start date.

Client

M2P Fintech — confirmed by the real copy embedded in the design. Whether this was a commissioned HR deliverable or a self-driven design made with real M2P content isn't confirmed.

My role

UX Designer, M2P Fintech — content, hierarchy, and interaction for one core screen.

Scope

One landing screen carrying a short, interactive hero carousel.

Evidence gap

The company is real and confirmed by the screen's own copy — but there's no research log, no usage data, and no confirmation of whether this shipped as an HR deliverable or stayed a design exploration. Everything below is read directly off the actual screen and its real copy, or cited from published secondary research, not reconstructed from a process that was never documented.

Stage 01
Context

The quiet gap between offer and Day 1

A new hire usually goes quiet for a stretch after signing an offer — no product to open, no team to see, just an HR email thread and a start date circled on a calendar. Preboarding treats that gap as a real design surface: not a form to fill out, but the first place a new hire can go to feel like they already belong somewhere.

The constraint was deliberately narrow: one screen, no login, no HR paperwork — just enough content to make the wait feel less blank.

Stage 02
Why It Was Made

What secondary research says about this gap

No primary research exists for this specific screen — but the gap it targets is well documented in published HR research. None of the numbers below were produced for this project; they're cited here to explain why a preboarding screen is a reasonable thing to design in the first place, not to claim this particular design was validated against them.

Brand story first, HR content second

The screen is built around a short, interactive hero carousel rather than a static welcome document — the two verified panels are a brand-identity moment ("Why 'Brand Name'?", with real M2P copy underneath) and a culture moment (Diversity & Inclusion). Both lead with a headline and a two-line description, followed by a single restrained "Learn more" — the screen states its point in one breath rather than front-loading a full handbook.

Evidence gap

These two figures are recreations, not screenshots — the source prototype renders through Figma's canvas with no exportable static image, so the layout and copy were reproduced by hand from what's visible in the live file. The headline text, body copy, and structure are real; exact colours, spacing, and typography are approximated, not pixel-accurate.

"We're committed to a collaborative, inclusive environment that encourages authenticity and fosters a sense of belonging."— actual copy, Diversity & Inclusion panel

That's a trade-off: depth for approachability. A single scrollable screen can't carry a full employee handbook, and it isn't trying to — it's optimised for someone reading on a phone, in a few idle minutes, weeks before they've met anyone at the company.

Evidence gap

No research or alternatives log exists for this specific concept — the priorities described above are read from the finished screen's own choices (carousel over document, two themes over a full handbook) and supported by the secondary research above, not from a documented internal process or usability testing on this design.

Stage 03
Ownership

One designer, one file, every decision

Preboarding was designed solo in Figma during the same M2P tenure as the Design System project on this site — there's no record of a team splitting the work, so content, layout, hierarchy, and the carousel's interaction pattern read as one person's calls. That also means there's no collaboration story to tell here: the honest version is a single-designer piece, and there's no confirmation of whether it moved beyond this Figma file into an actual HR handoff.

Stage 04
Impact

No deployment, no metrics — and that's the honest answer

There's no confirmation this screen was ever shipped to real M2P new hires, so there's no usage, retention, or satisfaction data to report — and inventing any would break the same rule every other case study on this site follows. What it does demonstrate is the pattern itself, backed by the retention research above: a single, content-led screen that a preboarding flow could realistically be built around, rather than a wireframe of an entire HR system.

Stage 05
Judgment

Choosing less, on purpose

The clearest judgment call here is what the screen leaves out. No account creation, no document uploads, no multi-step wizard — just two content panels and a single soft CTA. For a moment when a new hire has no obligation to be there yet, asking for less was the design decision, not a placeholder for more that never got built.

Key Learnings

A single screen can still carry a first impression
Preboarding never needed a full portal to make its point — one well-sequenced screen was enough to test whether the format works.
Content order is the real hierarchy decision
Leading with brand story and belonging, ahead of anything transactional, is itself the design decision — there's no layout trick that substitutes for picking what goes first.
Secondary research earns its place, if it's labelled
Citing real preboarding research explains why this screen is worth designing without pretending it was tested — the two are different claims, and conflating them would be the dishonest shortcut.
A concept still deserves an honest scope
It would have been easy to write this up as a fully commissioned product with invented metrics. Naming the real company, the real copy, and the real gaps — including what's unconfirmed — is the more useful version.
