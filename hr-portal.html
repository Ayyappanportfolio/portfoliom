HR Tech Onboarding Concept

Designing Preboarding

A single portal for the quiet weeks between accepting an offer and walking in on Day 1 — built to see what deserves to live there before the paperwork does.

Ayyappan Rajendiran UX Designer · Self-directed concept
Fig 01 — Preboarding: a single landing screen, designed for the gap before Day 1

Project Context

Preboarding is a concept exploration, not a client engagement — there's no named company behind it, and the interface itself uses a placeholder "Brand Name" rather than a real one. It asks a narrow question: between the moment someone accepts an offer and the moment they actually start, what's the one screen worth giving them?

Product

Preboarding — a portal for new hires to open before their start date.

Client

None — a self-directed concept, designed around a generic brand rather than a real one.

My role

Solo concept design in Figma — content, hierarchy, and interaction for one core screen.

Scope

One landing screen carrying a short, interactive hero carousel.

Evidence gap

This is a single-screen concept prototype — it was never commissioned, shipped, or tested with real new hires. There's no research log, no usage data, and no client to attribute a problem statement to. Everything below is read directly off the actual screen and its real copy, not reconstructed from a process that was never documented.

Stage 01
Context

The quiet gap between offer and Day 1

A new hire usually goes quiet for a stretch after signing an offer — no product to open, no team to see, just an HR email thread and a start date circled on a calendar. Preboarding treats that gap as a real design surface: not a form to fill out, but the first place a new hire can go to feel like they already belong somewhere.

The constraint was deliberately narrow: one screen, no login, no HR paperwork — just enough content to make the wait feel less blank.

Stage 02
Why It Was Made

Brand story first, HR content second

The screen is built around a short, interactive hero carousel rather than a static welcome document — the two verified panels are a brand-identity moment ("Why 'Brand Name'?") and a culture moment (Diversity & Inclusion). Both lead with a headline and a two-line description, followed by a single restrained "Learn more" — the screen states its point in one breath rather than front-loading a full handbook.

"We're committed to a collaborative, inclusive environment that encourages authenticity and fosters a sense of belonging."— actual copy, Diversity & Inclusion panel

That's a trade-off: depth for approachability. A single scrollable screen can't carry a full employee handbook, and it isn't trying to — it's optimised for someone reading on a phone, in a few idle minutes, weeks before they've met anyone at the company.

Evidence gap

No research or alternatives log exists for this concept — the priorities described above are read from the finished screen's own choices (carousel over document, two themes over a full handbook), not from a documented process.

Stage 03
Ownership

One designer, one file, every decision

Preboarding was designed solo in Figma — there was no team to divide the work across, so content, layout, hierarchy, and the carousel's interaction pattern were all the same person's call. That also means there's no collaboration story to tell here: the honest version is that this is a single-designer concept, built to explore a format rather than to ship a product.

Stage 04
Impact

No deployment, no metrics — and that's the honest answer

Preboarding was never commissioned, shipped, or handed to a real new hire, so there's no usage, retention, or satisfaction data to report — and inventing any would break the same rule every other case study on this site follows. What it does demonstrate is the pattern itself: a single, content-led screen that a preboarding flow could realistically be built around, rather than a wireframe of an entire HR system.

Stage 05
Judgment

Choosing less, on purpose

The clearest judgment call here is what the screen leaves out. No account creation, no document uploads, no multi-step wizard — just two content panels and a single soft CTA. For a moment when a new hire has no obligation to be there yet, asking for less was the design decision, not a placeholder for more that never got built.

Key Learnings

A single screen can still carry a first impression
Preboarding never needed a full portal to make its point — one well-sequenced screen was enough to test whether the format works.
Content order is the real hierarchy decision
Leading with brand story and belonging, ahead of anything transactional, is itself the design decision — there's no layout trick that substitutes for picking what goes first.
A concept still deserves an honest scope
It would have been easy to write this up as a full product with fabricated research and metrics. Naming it as a single-screen concept, with real gaps marked as gaps, is the more useful version.
