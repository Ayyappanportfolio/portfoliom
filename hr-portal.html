    Designing a Self-Service HR Portal

    Employees didn't distrust the tools — they distrusted the silence after using them. A full research-to-validation UX process, built around one insight: self-service was treated as a first attempt, never a final answer.

    Ayyappan Rajendiran · Senior Product Designer
    About this case study

    This is an illustrative, end-to-end practice case study — it demonstrates a full research-through-validation UX process, not a shipped client engagement. No company is named. Interview quotes, survey figures, usability-testing scores, and the final impact numbers are proposed placeholders written to be internally consistent, not real data from a live study — marked "illustrative" throughout rather than presented as fact.

    01Context

    An HR team drowning in questions the tools already answered

    An internal self-service app for a ~2,200-person company — leave requests, payslips, benefits enrollment, document access — already existed on paper. In practice, employees routed almost everything through email and shared inboxes instead: roughly 1,400 inbound HR requests a month, most of them repetitive lookups a tool was already built to answer. An HR team of six spent an estimated 60% of its time on request handling instead of policy work and onboarding design.

    Stakeholders

    VP of People, HR operations lead, Total Rewards manager, Legal/Compliance, 3 engineers, 1 PM, and me as senior product designer.

    Existing ecosystem

    A legacy payroll vendor with no employee UI, a separate benefits broker portal, and leave tracked in an HR-maintained spreadsheet.

    Constraints

    No write access to payroll's schema, a legal audit-trail requirement on every leave/benefits change, a 4-month timeline tied to open enrollment, and an immature design system.

    My role

    Owned UX end-to-end — research, IA, flows, wireframes, testing, and final UI.

    Timeline

    4 months: 3 weeks discovery, 5 weeks concepts/IA/flows, 5 weeks high-fidelity design in parallel with build, 3 weeks testing, 1 week launch prep.

    Success criteria

    Cut HR-request email volume 40%, get self-service completion above 90%, hold a launch SUS score above 80.

    02Research & Discovery

    Two competing hypotheses about why people still emailed

    HR leadership's starting theory was that employees simply didn't know the self-service tools existed. My working hypothesis was different: the deeper issue was trust — employees didn't believe an online form had actually updated payroll or their leave balance, so they emailed to be sure. The research plan was built to test both: 10 interviews across departments and tenure, 2 shadow sessions with HR ops processing inbound email, an employee survey, and a 90-day log analysis of the HR inbox.

    "I filled out the form, but I email anyway — I've been burned before when the balance was wrong."— interview participant (illustrative)
    "I didn't know there was a portal until my manager told me. It isn't linked from anywhere I actually look."— interview participant (illustrative)

    8 of 10 interview participants had used a self-service tool once, then reverted to email for the same task type later — consistently describing "not being sure it went through," not lack of awareness. A survey of 180 employees found 62% had emailed HR in the past 90 days; of those, 71% had already tried self-service for the same task at least once.

    • Pain points — no confirmation state after submitting, often-stale leave balances, and no single discoverable entry point.
    • Needs — a trustworthy, current source of truth; a visible confirmation on submission; one place to start.
    • Frictions — separate logins across payroll, benefits, and leave, with no clear sense of which system owned what.
    • Workarounds — some employees kept personal spreadsheets as a cross-check against HR's own numbers.
    • Contradictory finding — 2 of 10 wanted a documented human reply for anything pay-related regardless of tooling — this kept an "Ask HR" escalation path visible everywhere, not just as a fallback.
    Research limitations (illustrative)

    The sample skewed toward employees who'd already emailed HR in the last 90 days, sourced via HR's own referral list — findings likely undercount employees who were already fully self-sufficient.

    04Problem & Opportunity

    The journey's worst moment is evaluation, not search

    Mapping the highest-volume task — checking a leave balance — showed trust dropping hardest right after the number appears on screen, not while searching for the tool or logging in.

    Fig 01 — Illustrative emotional curve, the leave-balance check task, from the journey map
    SymptomHR ops is overwhelmed with repetitive email requests
    ProblemEmployees don't trust self-service enough to treat it as final
    Root causeThe tools complete the transaction but never signal it's trustworthy or final — no confirmation, no freshness indicator, no single entry point
    Point of view

    Priya needs a visibly trustworthy, single-entry-point way to complete routine HR tasks, because the current tools complete the transaction without ever confirming it — leaving enough doubt that email feels safer.

    • How might we make every self-service action feel definitively complete?
    • How might we make data freshness visible without the employee having to ask?
    • How might we give employees one place to start, regardless of which backend system holds the answer?
    • How might we preserve a human escalation path without it becoming the default?

    Priority matrix — impact × effort

    Why the chatbot was rejected

    An HR FAQ chatbot was considered and dropped: the research pointed to a trust gap in an already-completed transaction, not to missing information. A chatbot added a new surface without closing the confirmation gap — and risked employees trusting a bot's answer on pay data even less than a static number.

    05Ideation & Information Architecture

    Four principles, then a real IA — not a jump to the UI

    • Confirm, don't imply — every write action gets an explicit, persistent confirmation.
    • Show your work — every number carries a timestamp and a source.
    • One front door — a single, memorable entry point regardless of the backend system behind it.
    • Escalation is a feature — keep a human path visible without making it the default.

    For the confirmation pattern, three directions were sketched: a toast plus email receipt (simple, but easy to miss); a persistent "My Requests" status tracker (durable, reusable across every request type); and a chat-style confirmation thread engineering floated as reuse of an existing notifications component. The chat thread was rejected — it overlapped the email channel it was meant to replace, with no evidence anyone wanted more messaging. The shipped answer combined the first two: an immediate toast plus a persistent tracker.

    Before

    Links scattered across three systems' own logins, no unifying navigation. A leave balance meant a link to a spreadsheet or a payroll site buried three clicks deep.

    After

    One entry point. Navigation grouped by task domain — Leave, Payslips, Benefits, My Requests, Ask HR, Profile — never by which backend system owns it.

    Fig 02 — After: navigation grouped by task domain, not by backend system

    "My Requests" was chosen over "Activity" because employees described the need as "did my request go through" — never an abstract log. No formal card sort was run; the grouping follows the task-domain language ("my leave," "my payslip") used unprompted across the interviews.

    06User Flows & Wireframes

    Validate before submission, not after

    Research showed employees distrust systems that let them submit something invalid and find out later — so the critical "request time off" flow checks the balance and flags problems before the review step, not after.

    Select leave type→ Select dates→ Balance check→ Review & confirm→ Pending in My Requests→ Approved / Denied
    • Error state — insufficient balance blocks submission but suggests the maximum days actually available.
    • Empty state — a first-time My Requests view explains itself instead of showing a blank table.
    • Edge case — a public holiday inside the range is auto-excluded from the count before submit; a request beyond the remaining balance is flagged, not blocked, since policy allows manager-approved negative balances.

    Wireframe progression — leave request form

    Fig 03 — Illustrative progression: low, mid, and high fidelity, leave request form
    • Inline validation — fires on blur, not on every keystroke, so a date range in progress isn't flagged mid-entry.
    • Loading — skeleton screens for balance and My Requests, which depend on a 1–2 second payroll API lag — reduced perceived wait in testing.
    • Accessibility — status is never color alone; 44px touch targets; form errors announced through ARIA live regions.
    07Ownership & Collaboration

    Reprioritizing the roadmap before a single wireframe existed

    HR leadership's original ask was a richer, more detailed request form. I brought the interview and log-analysis evidence to a working session and got agreement to reprioritize toward the confirmation and freshness work instead — the form redesign still happened, just after, not before. I also pushed back on engineering's proposal to reuse an existing chat-style notifications component for confirmations, with the pros/cons comparison from ideation; and I owned the IA restructuring from three siloed systems into one domain-based navigation, negotiating a single sign-on landing page with IT.

    • With the PM — the quick-wins-before-strategic-bets sequencing came from my priority matrix and was adopted directly as the sprint plan.
    • With engineering — a joint SSO feasibility pass before final wireframes surfaced the identity-provider dependency early enough to rescope it into a later sprint instead of blocking launch.
    • With Legal — the audit-trail requirement's UI implications — a visible, timestamped status history — became one design solution that satisfied compliance and closed the trust gap at once.

    Mid-project, the payroll vendor's API turned out to have a 1–2 second response lag unknown during ideation. Rather than ship a spinner — which testing later showed read as "is this broken?" — I adjusted to skeleton screens and worked with engineering to prefetch balance data on login where possible.

    08Final Design & Impact

    One entry point, one status trail, every number sourced

    The shipped portal has a single sign-on entry point, five domain-based sections, and an always-visible "Ask HR" action. Every write action surfaces an immediate toast, a status entry in My Requests, and a timestamped audit-trail entry that satisfies Legal's requirement. A status-badge component (color, icon, and text together) and a timestamp/source micro-pattern — "Synced from Payroll System · updated 2 hours ago" — were extended across every screen pulling from a backend system, and later became the template for the benefits-enrollment flow too.

    • One entry point instead of three disconnected logins.
    • Every number carries a source and a freshness timestamp.
    • Every write action produces a persistent, checkable status, not just a momentary toast.
    • A visible escalation path preserved for the minority who want one.

    09Usability Testing & Validation

    The trust gap survived into the first prototype

    Round 1 tested the mid-fidelity prototype with 6 employees against 3 tasks: check leave balance, submit a leave request, and find the status of a request submitted "yesterday." The most important finding: the confirmation toast auto-dismissed before 2 of 6 participants read it — the exact trust gap this project was meant to fix had survived into the interactive prototype.

    What improved
    Confirmation persistence and the inline timestamp fully resolved both round 1 critical/major issues — no round 2 participant second-guessed a submission.
    What surprised us
    Confidence rose more than completion did. Some round 1 participants completed tasks but still rated confidence low — which validated tracking confidence as its own metric, not just completion.
    What remains unresolved
    A minor misread of the negative-balance copy persisted for one participant. Several also asked, unprompted, about seeing a teammate's coverage during leave — flagged for a future phase, not built here.
    What's next
    A phase exploring team visibility during leave, and extending the timestamp/source pattern to benefits enrollment, which reused it but wasn't separately tested.
    Research→ Insight→ Problem→ Opportunity→ Priority→ IA→ Flow→ Design→ Test→ Iterate→ Validate

    The project moved from a stated symptom — HR ops overwhelmed by repetitive email — to a validated root cause: a trust gap at the moment of submission and data display. The shipped design closed that gap: 94% self-service completion, a 54% drop in HR-request email volume, and a launch SUS score of 84.

    10Key Learnings

    Key Learnings

    Trust is a design problem, not a copy problem
    The fix wasn't better forms — it was making every action visibly, persistently confirmed. Simplifying the interface would have missed the actual failure point entirely.
    Evidence beats seniority in a prioritization fight
    Leadership's original ask was reasonable on its face. Bringing interview and log data to the table, rather than asserting a different opinion, is what moved the roadmap.
    A metric can hide the real problem
    Task completion alone would have called round 1 a success. Adding a confidence rating caught the trust gap that completion rate couldn't see.
    Illustrative work still deserves an honest label
    This case study demonstrates process rigor — research through validation — without claiming to be a real client engagement. Marking that clearly is the more useful version.
